1. Introduction and why interpolation

Interpolation is a numerical technique used to estimate values of a function at points that are not directly available from the data. In experimental physics and scientific computing, measurements are often available only at discrete sample points, while the underlying behavior is continuous. Interpolation helps reconstruct a smooth curve between known values for analysis, visualization, and simulation.

It is especially useful when many physical quantities are observed only at selected positions or times. Instead of treating the data as isolated points, interpolation provides a reasonable approximation of the function between them.

1.1 Lagrange interpolation

Lagrange interpolation constructs a polynomial that passes through all available data points. For points (x0, y0), (x1, y1), …, (xn, yn), the interpolation polynomial can be written as

P(x) = Σ yi ∏(x - xj)/(xi - xj)

This method is simple and exact at the given nodes, but for many points the resulting polynomial can become unstable and oscillatory.

1.2 Newton interpolation

Newton interpolation expresses the polynomial in a basis of nested factors such as

P(x) = a0 + a1(x - x0) + a2(x - x0)(x - x1) + ...

The coefficients are obtained from divided differences, which makes the method convenient when new data points are added incrementally.

1.3 Cubic spline interpolation

Cubic spline interpolation uses piecewise cubic polynomials between neighboring data points. It enforces continuity of the function and its first and second derivatives at the knots, giving a smooth and stable curve.

This is often preferred over a single high-degree polynomial when the goal is a visually smooth and physically reasonable approximation.

1.4 Runge's phenomenon

Runge's phenomenon shows that increasing the degree of an interpolating polynomial can lead to large oscillations near the boundaries when equally spaced nodes are used. A classical example is the function f(x) = 1 / (1 + x^2) on [-5, 5].

The notebook Code/runge_interpolation_comparison.ipynb compares several approaches and illustrates why piecewise and spline-based methods are often more stable.
